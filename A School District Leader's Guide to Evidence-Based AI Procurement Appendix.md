## **Framework Mapping and Selection Rationale**

### **National Institute of Standards and Technology AI Risk Management Framework**

Developed by the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), part of the U.S. Department of Commerce, the AI RMF is a voluntary framework for helping organizations identify, assess, and manage AI risks. Developed through collaboration with public- and private-sector stakeholders, it provides a recognized foundation for organizing trustworthy-AI considerations and risk-management activities.

The AI RMF serves as the primary conceptual foundation for the toolkit. Its trustworthy-AI characteristics informed the substantive areas represented in the framework, while its Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage functions informed the broader evaluation process. The toolkit operationalizes these concepts for K–12 procurement through eight Trustworthiness Dimensions, district-facing Requirements, evidence expectations, and a structured Requirement Determination process.

**Source Influences:**

* *Section 1.1, Characteristics of Trustworthy AI Systems:* Informs the selection of substantive trustworthiness areas, including validity and reliability, safety, security and resilience, accountability and transparency, explainability and interpretability, privacy, and fairness.

* *Section 2, AI RMF Core:* The Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage functions informs the overall approach to contextualizing the use case, identifying relevant risks, evaluating evidence, and determining appropriate action.

* *Adaptation for K–12 Procurement:* The toolkit extends this general-purpose framework with education-specific considerations and operational guidance for evaluating vendor claims and supporting adoption decisions.

### **EDSAFE AI SAFE Framework**

The EDSAFE AI Alliance is a cross-sector initiative founded in 2020 and led by InnovateEDU, bringing together organizations and stakeholders across education and the workforce. Its work focuses specifically on developing a safer, more equitable, and more trustworthy AI ecosystem for education. The SAFE Framework was developed to synthesize existing AI safety, trust, and governance approaches into guidance relevant to educational policy and practice. EDSAFE reports that the framework draws on more than 24 global AI safety, trust, and market frameworks.

The framework provides a shared structure for identifying and addressing the risks and responsibilities associated with AI in education. It is intended to support policymakers, education leaders, technology providers, and other stakeholders in translating broad principles into policies and implementation practices. Its four central areas are Safety, Accountability, Fairness, and Efficacy; EDSAFE's current materials also emphasize transparency and effective use.

EDSAFE was used to ensure that the framework remained grounded in the realities of educational AI rather than simply adapting a general-purpose technology framework to schools. Its emphasis on safety, accountability, fairness, and efficacy informed corresponding areas of the eight dimensions. The toolkit extends these broad concepts by separating concerns that require distinct procurement questions, such as system performance, privacy, security, explainability, and human oversight. This allows the framework to retain EDSAFE's education-specific orientation while providing greater operational specificity for district evaluation.

**Source Influences:**

* *Safety:* Informs the toolkit’s emphasis on protecting students and their data, addressing harmful outputs, and considering privacy and cybersecurity risks.

* *Accountability:* Supports expectations for defined responsibilities, governance, and clear responsibility for AI-related decisions and outcomes.

* *Fairness and Transparency:* Informs the toolkit’s focus on bias, equitable outcomes, accessibility, and making relevant information available to those evaluating a system.

* *Efficacy:* Supports the expectation that AI tools demonstrate educational value rather than relying solely on vendor claims or technological novelty.

### **National Institute of Standards and Technology Privacy Framework**

The NIST Privacy Framework is a voluntary, risk- and outcome-based framework designed to help organizations identify and manage privacy risks while using data and developing products and services. It is technology-, sector-, and jurisdiction-neutral, making it adaptable to different organizational settings, including K–12 education. Its intended users include organizations responsible for personal data, privacy professionals, risk managers, and organizational leaders.

The NIST Privacy Framework serves as a primary source for the toolkit’s Privacy & Data Governance dimension. It complements the NIST AI RMF by providing a more detailed structure for identifying and managing privacy risks associated with data processing. The toolkit applies this structure to K–12 AI procurement by guiding districts to examine what student and educator data a system collects, how those data are used and shared, how long they are retained, and what safeguards govern their processing. It also informs expectations for documented privacy policies, clear responsibilities, appropriate access controls, and ongoing monitoring. These concepts are translated into district-facing requirements, evidence expectations, and vendor questions to help districts assess whether privacy protections are adequately supported by evidence.

**Source Influence:**

* *Identify-P:* Informs questions about what personal data the system collects, how data flow between the vendor and third parties, and what privacy risks may arise from those practices.

* *Govern-P*: Supports expectations for documented privacy policies, defined responsibilities, and processes for overseeing and reviewing privacy risks.

* *Control-P*: Informs evaluation of how personal data are managed throughout processing, including whether data handling practices appropriately limit or control their use.

* *Communicate-P:* Informs expectations that districts can understand how personal data are processed and what privacy risks and practices are relevant to their decisions.

* *Protect-P*: Informs evaluation of safeguards for personal data, including identity management, authentication, access controls, and data security.

### **MITRE Adversarial Threat Landscape for AI Systems**

MITRE is a nonprofit organization with established expertise in cybersecurity research and threat-informed defense. Its Adversarial Threat Landscape for AI Systems (ATLAS) is a publicly accessible, continuously updated knowledge base developed through collaboration across industry, academia, and government. It documents adversarial tactics and techniques targeting AI-enabled systems, drawing on observed attacks and realistic demonstrations from AI red teams and security groups. This makes ATLAS a relevant technical source for understanding AI-specific security threats, although it is not an education-specific procurement framework.

MITRE ATLAS serves as a primary technical resource for the toolkit’s Security & Resiliency dimension. It complements the NIST AI RMF’s broader risk-management approach and OWASP’s application-level security guidance by providing a structured account of adversarial behaviors targeting AI systems. The toolkit uses ATLAS to inform the evaluation of AI-specific threats, including manipulation of inputs, prompt injection, data or model poisoning, unauthorized access, and attempts to extract sensitive information. These threats help shape the security evidence districts should seek from vendors, including threat assessments, security testing results, documented mitigations, and processes for detecting and responding to attacks.

**Source Influence:**

* *ATLAS Matrix — Tactics and Techniques:* Informs consideration of how adversaries may target AI systems across different stages of an attack, helping districts identify relevant threats based on a product’s architecture and intended use.

* *LLM Prompt Injection and LLM Jailbreak:* Inform questions about whether a system can be manipulated through crafted inputs to bypass safeguards, produce harmful outputs, or take actions beyond its intended boundaries.

* *Training Data Poisoning and RAG Poisoning:* Inform evaluation of risks that malicious or manipulated training data or retrieved content could compromise system behavior or output integrity.

* *AI Model Access and Exfiltration-related techniques:* Inform questions about controls governing access to models, connected services, and sensitive information, including student data.

* *Mitigations*: Inform requests for documented safeguards and evidence that vendors have tested their effectiveness against relevant threats.

* *Case Studies:* Provide concrete examples of attacks against AI-enabled systems that can help evaluators understand realistic threat scenarios and inform vendor security questions.

### **Open Worldwide Application Security Project**

Open Worldwide Application Security Project (OWASP) was incorporated to provide additional technical specificity concerning security risks associated with large language models and generative AI The Open Worldwide Application Security Project (OWASP) is a nonprofit, open-source initiative focused on improving software and application security. Its GenAI Security Project brings together security practitioners, researchers, developers, and other contributors to identify and document vulnerabilities in generative AI systems. The OWASP Top 10 for Large Language Model Applications synthesizes these risks into actionable guidance for developers and organizations evaluating or deploying LLM-based applications. Its relevance comes from its specialized application-security focus and community-driven technical contributions, rather than from being a K–12-specific or regulatory framework.

OWASP serves as a primary technical resource for the toolkit’s Security & Resiliency dimension. It complements NIST AI RMF’s broader risk-management structure and MITRE ATLAS’s threat-informed knowledge base by providing concrete vulnerability categories and mitigation guidance for LLM applications. The toolkit applies these resources to K–12 procurement by informing questions about prompt injection, sensitive information disclosure, third-party components, data and model poisoning, insecure output handling, and excessive system permissions or autonomy. These risks help districts identify what security documentation, testing results, access controls, and mitigation evidence to request from vendors. The toolkit uses OWASP to guide risk identification and evidence requests; the presence of a documented mitigation alone does not establish that a system is secure.

**Source Influence:**

* *LLM02: Sensitive Information Disclosure:* Informs evaluation of safeguards against exposing student information, educator data, confidential records, or other sensitive information through system outputs.

* *LLM03: Supply Chain:* Informs questions about third-party models, datasets, software components, and services, including how vendors assess and manage risks introduced by external dependencies.

* *LLM04: Data and Model Poisoning:* Informs consideration of whether manipulated training, fine-tuning, or embedding data could compromise system integrity, introduce vulnerabilities, or distort outputs.

* *LLM05: Improper Output Handling:* Informs questions about whether AI-generated outputs are validated and sanitized before being passed to other systems or used to trigger actions.

* *LLM06: Excessive Agency:* Informs evaluation of whether an AI system has more functionality, permissions, or autonomy than necessary, and whether consequential actions are appropriately restricted.

* *LLM08: Vector and Embedding Weaknesses:* Informs questions about risks associated with retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), including weaknesses in how information is stored, retrieved, or used to generate responses.

### **United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization Recommendations on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence**

The United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization (UNESCO) is a specialized United Nations agency with an international mandate covering education, science, culture, and information. Its Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence was adopted by UNESCO’s 193rd General Conference on November 23, 2021, and is its first global standard-setting instrument on AI ethics. Developed through an international, multistakeholder process, it establishes a human-rights-based approach to AI governance. Its relevance to this toolkit is particularly strong because it explicitly addresses education, children and young people, human oversight, fairness, transparency, and the social implications of AI.

The Recommendation provides ethical principles and policy guidance for the responsible development, deployment, and governance of AI throughout its lifecycle. It is addressed primarily to UNESCO Member States and policymakers, while also offering guidance to public- and private-sector AI actors. Its policy action areas include education and research, data governance, and human rights, helping governments and institutions translate broad ethical principles into practical policies and oversight mechanisms.

The UNESCO Recommendation serves as a supplementary source for the toolkit’s education-specific ethical and governance considerations. It complements the NIST AI RMF and EDSAFE AI SAFE Framework by grounding responsible AI procurement in human rights, student well-being, equity, and the preservation of human agency. The toolkit draws on UNESCO’s guidance to inform expectations for fairness, transparency, accountability, privacy, and meaningful human oversight. Its education-specific recommendations also reinforce the importance of assessing AI’s effects on students and educators, protecting learners’ data, and ensuring that AI supports rather than undermines educational relationships and learning. These principles help shape the toolkit’s district-facing requirements and vendor questions, particularly where districts must consider not only whether a system functions as intended, but also whether its use is appropriate for students and the educational context.

**Source Influence:**

* *Human Oversight and Determination (Sections 35–36):* Informs the toolkit’s Human Oversight & Agency dimension by reinforcing that AI must not replace ultimate human responsibility and that people must retain meaningful authority over consequential decisions.

* *Transparency and Explainability (Sections 37–41):* Informs expectations that vendors provide relevant information about system behavior and outputs, with explanations appropriate to the context and potential impact of the system.

* *Responsibility and Accountability (Sections 42–43):* Supports requirements for clearly assigned responsibilities, oversight mechanisms, impact assessments, and the ability to audit and trace AI systems and their effects.

* *Data Policy (Section 71):* Informs expectations for evaluating training-data quality, data collection and selection practices, data security, and mechanisms for learning from errors.

* *Education and Research (Sections 101–106):* Informs the toolkit’s K–12-specific considerations, including AI literacy, assessment of impacts on students and teachers, protection of learner data, equitable participation, and ensuring that AI supports educational experiences and relationships.

* *Fairness and Non-Discrimination (Core Principles):* Informs the Fairness & Bias Mitigation dimension by emphasizing social justice, non-discrimination, inclusion, and equitable access to AI’s benefits.

* *Privacy and Data Protection (Section 34):* Supports expectations for privacy impact assessments and protection of personal information throughout the AI system lifecycle.

### **Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act and Children's Online Privacy Protection Rules**

FERPA (Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act) is a U.S. federal law enacted in 1974 and administered by the U.S. Department of Education. The Department’s Student Privacy Policy Office provides official guidance on the protection of education records, disclosure requirements, and the responsibilities of schools and third-party service providers. COPPA (Children’s Online Privacy Protection Act) is a U.S. federal law enacted in 1998 and enforced by the Federal Trade Commission (FTC), which issues the Children’s Online Privacy Protection Rule and guidance on children’s online privacy. Both are authoritative legal sources for understanding student privacy obligations, although their protections apply to different types of information, entities, and circumstances.

FERPA protects the privacy of education records at educational agencies and institutions covered by the law. It establishes rights concerning access to and amendment of records, as well as requirements governing the disclosure of personally identifiable information (PII) from those records. Its guidance is intended for schools, districts, postsecondary institutions, and other parties handling education records.

COPPA regulates covered websites and online services that collect personal information online from children under 13\. It establishes requirements concerning privacy notices, verifiable parental consent, parental access and deletion rights, data security, and data retention. Its guidance is directed primarily at online service operators, while also explaining how schools may authorize certain educational technology services to collect children’s information for school purposes.

FERPA and COPPA serve as primary legal sources for the toolkit’s Privacy & Data Governance dimension. They help translate existing student privacy protections into practical procurement considerations for K–12 AI systems. FERPA informs expectations concerning vendors’ access to education records, their permitted use and disclosure of student information, and the school’s control over third-party providers. COPPA informs expectations concerning the collection and use of personal information from children under 13, parental consent and notice, data minimization, security, and retention and deletion practices. Together, these laws help shape district-facing requirements and vendor questions about data collection, authorized use, third-party sharing, training on student data, and contractual safeguards. The toolkit uses them as legal reference points, while recognizing that their applicability depends on the specific system, data, entities, and circumstances. Compliance with either law alone does not establish that an AI system satisfies all of a district’s privacy and governance needs.

**Source Influence:**

**FERPA**

* *Definition of education records and personally identifiable information:* Informs questions about whether a system collects, accesses, generates, or maintains information that qualifies as protected education-record information.

* *School Official Exception (34 CFR § 99.31(a)(1)(i)(B)):* Informs expectations for evaluating whether a vendor receiving education records performs an authorized institutional function, remains under the school’s direct control regarding use and maintenance of records, and meets the applicable school-official requirements.

* *Use and Redisclosure Restrictions (34 CFR § 99.33):* Informs questions about whether vendors restrict student information to authorized purposes and prevent unauthorized disclosure or reuse.

* *Access and Amendment Rights (34 CFR §§ 99.10–99.20):* Informs consideration of how student records maintained by a vendor can remain accessible and correctable in accordance with applicable FERPA requirements.

COPPA

* *Notice and Verifiable Parental Consent (16 CFR §§ 312.4–312.5):* Informs questions about what information a vendor collects from children under 13, how its practices are disclosed, and what consent mechanism applies.

* *School Authorization for Educational Use (FTC COPPA FAQs, Section N):* Informs questions about whether school-authorized collection is limited to the educational context and whether information is used for the benefit of the school rather than for the vendor’s independent commercial purposes.

* *Confidentiality, Security, and Integrity (16 CFR § 312.8):* Informs evaluation of safeguards protecting children’s personal information against unauthorized access, use, or disclosure.

* *Data Retention and Deletion (16 CFR § 312.10):* Informs questions about how long children’s information is retained, when it is deleted, and what safeguards govern deletion.

* *Data Minimization (16 CFR § 312.7):* Informs questions about whether a service requires children to provide more personal information than is reasonably necessary for the activity.

## **The Eight Trustworthiness Dimensions In Detail**

### **Validity, Reliability, & Robustness**

#### **Definition**

Validity, Reliability & Robustness refers to whether an AI system performs its intended function accurately and consistently across relevant users, tasks, and conditions, including realistic variation and unexpected or imperfect inputs.

For K–12 procurement, districts should assess whether an AI system can perform the specific educational task for which it is being considered, rather than relying solely on general benchmarks or product demonstrations. Performance should be examined across the students, educators, settings, and input conditions relevant to the intended use. Districts should also consider whether performance remains adequate when conditions differ from those used in testing and whether the vendor monitors meaningful changes in performance over time.

**Example**: A district is considering an AI writing-feedback tool for middle school students. The vendor provides strong results from a general language benchmark and demonstrates the product using polished sample essays. However, the district expects students with varying writing proficiency to submit short, incomplete, and inconsistently structured assignments.

The district therefore needs evidence that the system performs adequately on writing samples and conditions representative of its intended use. Strong general benchmark results or a successful product demonstration would not, by themselves, establish that the system can reliably provide the type of feedback the district intends to use.

#### **Checklist Requirements**

1) *The system performs its intended educational function accurately.*

**What this means:** The district should determine whether the system can perform the specific educational task for which it is being considered. Evaluation should be based on a clearly defined performance standard appropriate to that task, rather than on general measures of model capability.

2) *The system performs consistently across relevant users, populations, and contexts.*

**What this means:** Performance should be examined across the students, educators, grade levels, subjects, and instructional settings relevant to the intended deployment. Aggregate results may obscure meaningful differences across populations or contexts, so districts should determine whether performance remains adequate across the populations and settings for which the system is intended.

3) *The system maintains adequate performance under realistic and varied input conditions.*

**What this means:** AI systems may encounter inputs that differ from controlled demonstrations or curated evaluation datasets. Districts should consider whether the system maintains adequate performance when inputs are incomplete, ambiguous, unusual, inconsistent, or otherwise imperfect in ways that are reasonably likely to occur during actual use.

Robustness does not require identical performance under every possible condition. Instead, the district should understand where performance may degrade, how likely those conditions are to occur, and whether resulting failures are consequential for the intended educational use.

4) *The vendor monitors and addresses meaningful changes in system performance over time.*

**What this means**: AI systems may change because of model updates, changes to prompts or retrieval systems, new data, or other product modifications. The district should determine whether the vendor has a process for identifying meaningful changes or regressions in performance and addressing them when they occur.

The district should also consider whether the system evaluated during procurement is sufficiently representative of the version and configuration it would actually procure.

#### **Evidence to Obtain**

To evaluate the requirements above, the district should seek information that establishes how well the system performs for its intended educational use and under conditions relevant to deployment. Relevant evidence may include:

* **Task-specific performance and errors:** Appropriate performance metrics, error rates, common failure modes, and their potential consequences for the intended use.

* **Performance across populations and conditions:** Results across relevant student or educator populations, task types, instructional settings, and challenging inputs.

* **Robustness and consistency:** Testing with incomplete, ambiguous, contradictory, or otherwise imperfect inputs, and evidence of output consistency across repeated or equivalent inputs.

* **Evaluation design and data:** Evaluation sample size and composition, data provenance, representativeness, and whether test data were held out from model development or tuning.

* **Benchmarks and uncertainty:** Comparisons with appropriate performance standards, human baselines, or alternatives, alongside relevant measures of statistical uncertainty.

* **Independent validation:** External evaluation or replication, including who conducted it and whether the methods and conditions reflect the intended use.

* **System-version correspondence:** Documentation of the model, product version, configuration, and other material components evaluated, and whether they match the system proposed for procurement.

* **Ongoing monitoring and change management:** Performance monitoring, regression testing, material change records, and processes for investigating and addressing performance degradation.

### **Safety**

#### **Definition**

Safety refers to whether an AI system can prevent or minimize foreseeable harm resulting from its use, including harmful outputs, unsafe interactions, and other risks to users.

For K–12 procurement, districts should assess whether an AI system has safeguards appropriate for student use, whether foreseeable harms associated with the intended use have been identified and evaluated, and whether those safeguards have been tested under realistic conditions. Districts should also determine whether the vendor has a defined process for responding to safety incidents and monitoring safety risks over time. The existence of a general safety policy or safeguard does not, by itself, establish that the system is safe for a particular educational use.

**Example:** A district is considering an AI chatbot that students will use for homework support. The vendor states that the system has content moderation and “child-safe” protections but provides limited information about how those safeguards were designed or tested. The district expects students to ask unexpected questions, attempt to bypass restrictions, and interact with the system repeatedly.

The district therefore needs evidence that the safeguards address foreseeable risks for the intended student population and that the system has been tested under realistic student-use conditions. A general safety policy or the presence of a content filter would not, by itself, demonstrate that the system adequately prevents or minimizes harmful outputs.

#### **Checklist Requirements**

1) *The system has safeguards designed to prevent harmful or inappropriate outputs, including protections relevant to minors.*

**What this means:** The district should determine whether the system includes concrete safeguards addressing harmful or inappropriate outputs that are reasonably foreseeable for the intended educational use. These may include protections against sexual content, violence, harassment, self-harm content, or other age-inappropriate material, depending on the system and use case. General-purpose safety controls should not be assumed to address the specific risks of student use.

2) *The system includes safeguards appropriate for use by minors and the ages of students who will use it.*

**What this means:** Student users may have different vulnerabilities, developmental needs, and patterns of interaction than adults. The district should determine whether safety protections are appropriate for the specific age groups and roles that will use the system and whether the vendor has evaluated those protections with relevant student populations.

3) *The vendor evaluates foreseeable physical, psychological, social, or educational harms associated with the intended use.*

**What this means:** Safety evaluation should begin with identifying how the system could cause or contribute to harm in its intended educational context. Potential harms may extend beyond unsafe content to inappropriate recommendations, harmful feedback, student distress, social consequences, or negative effects on learning. The district should determine whether the vendor has systematically identified relevant harms and used those risks to inform safeguards, testing, or other design decisions.

4) *The vendor has tested the system for harmful or inappropriate outputs under realistic student-use conditions.*

**What this means:** Safety claims should be supported by testing that reflects how students are likely to interact with the system. Where relevant, testing should include unexpected, ambiguous, repeated, or deliberately challenging interactions and attempts to circumvent safety controls. Districts should examine both the results of testing and the limitations or failure modes identified.

5) *The vendor provides a defined process for identifying, reporting, investigating, and responding to unsafe outputs or incidents.*

**What this means:** Even well-designed safeguards cannot guarantee that unsafe outputs will never occur. The district should therefore determine whether the vendor has a clear process for reporting and escalating safety incidents, investigating their causes, addressing confirmed failures, and communicating material incidents to affected districts. Responsibilities between the vendor and district should be sufficiently clear for the district to act when an incident occurs.

6) *The vendor monitors safety performance after deployment and communicates significant safety issues or newly identified risks to the district.*

**What this means:** Safety risks can change as models, system components, safeguards, or patterns of use change. The district should determine whether the vendor continuously monitors safety performance, evaluates newly identified risks, reassesses safeguards after material system changes, and has a defined mechanism for communicating significant safety issues to customers.

#### **Evidence to Obtain**

To evaluate the requirements above, the district should seek information that demonstrates whether the system's safety protections address foreseeable risks for the intended educational use and whether those protections function effectively in practice. Relevant evidence may include:

* **Safety safeguards and controls:** Documentation of content filters, moderation systems, safety policies, refusal mechanisms, age restrictions, blocking controls, or other technical safeguards used to prevent or reduce harmful outputs. The district should identify which safeguards are implemented in the specific product and configuration being evaluated.

* **Age-appropriate protections:** Documentation describing protections for minors and supported age groups, including age-specific configurations, restrictions, escalation mechanisms, or other safeguards. Where relevant, evidence should show how protections differ across age groups or user types.

* **Risk and harm assessment:** Documented assessments of foreseeable physical, psychological, social, and educational harms associated with the intended use, including identified failure modes, foreseeable misuse scenarios, hazard or risk analyses, and evidence showing how identified risks informed safeguards or testing.

* **Safety testing methodology:** Documentation of safety test cases, evaluation methodology, test conditions, and coverage of harmful or inappropriate outputs relevant to the intended use. Where applicable, this should include adversarial, misuse, or red-team testing.

* **Safety performance results:** Results showing the frequency and types of unsafe or inappropriate outputs identified during testing, including failure rates, successful safeguard-intervention rates, categories of harmful outputs, and performance across relevant test conditions. Where quantitative metrics are unavailable or inappropriate, the district should seek documented examples and systematic failure analysis.

* **Realistic student-use testing:** Evidence from testing that reflects realistic student interactions, including unexpected prompts, ambiguous requests, repeated interactions, age-appropriate scenarios, and attempts to circumvent safety controls where relevant. Districts should examine whether testing conditions meaningfully represent the proposed deployment.

* **Known safety failures and limitations:** Documentation of known safety failures, recurring unsafe behaviors, limitations of existing safeguards, conditions that trigger failures, and the severity and potential consequences of those failures.

* **Independent or third-party testing:** External evaluations, red-team exercises, audits, or other independent testing of safety protections where available. The district should consider who conducted the evaluation, what was tested, and whether the testing was relevant to the intended use.

* **Safety incident history:** Records or summaries of material safety incidents, recurring harmful outputs, or other significant safety failures, where available, including the nature of the incident, response, corrective action, and whether similar failures were subsequently observed.

* **Incident response procedures:** Documentation describing how safety incidents are reported, investigated, escalated, remediated, and communicated. This may include reporting channels, responsible parties, escalation procedures, response-time commitments where applicable, and district notification procedures.

* **Ongoing safety monitoring:** Evidence of post-deployment monitoring, including safety metrics or indicators tracked, monitoring frequency, ongoing testing or red-teaming, processes for identifying emerging risks, and procedures for updating safeguards in response to identified problems.

* **Change-related safety evaluation:** Evidence showing how safety protections are reevaluated following material model, system, safeguard, or configuration changes, including comparative testing where appropriate.

If relevant information is unavailable, the district should seek additional documentation, clarification, testing, or validation before determining whether the applicable requirements are satisfied.

#### **Questions to Ask Vendors**

The following questions can help the district obtain and interpret evidence relevant to this dimension:

**Safeguards and Age-Appropriate Protection**

* What safeguards are used to prevent or reduce harmful or inappropriate outputs in the product?

* What categories of harmful or age-inappropriate content does the system detect, restrict, or refuse?

* Which protections are specifically designed for minors?

* Which age groups has the product been designed and tested for, and do safety controls differ across age groups?

* What evidence demonstrates that these safeguards function as intended in the product and configuration we would procure?

**Foreseeable Harms and Risk Assessment**

* What physical, psychological, social, or educational harms have you identified as foreseeable for this product and intended use?

* How were those risks identified and assessed?

* Which risks are specific to student users or the educational context?

* What safeguards, design decisions, or other mitigations were implemented in response to the risks identified?

**Safety Testing and Failure Modes**

* What types of harmful or unsafe outputs have you tested for, and how were the test cases developed?

* Were tests conducted using realistic student interactions, including unexpected, ambiguous, repeated, or deliberately challenging prompts?

* Did testing include attempts to circumvent or bypass safety controls where relevant?

* What safety failures or unsafe outputs were identified, how frequently did they occur, and under what conditions?

* What changes or mitigations were made in response to failures identified during testing?

**Incident Response and Monitoring**

* How can a district report a harmful output or other safety incident, and who is responsible for investigating it?

* How are serious or recurring safety issues escalated, investigated, and remediated?

* What commitments does the vendor make regarding response and notification when a significant safety incident affects district users?

* How do you monitor safety performance after deployment?

* How are newly identified risks detected and evaluated?

* What triggers additional safety testing, mitigation, or reevaluation of existing safeguards?

* How are districts notified about significant safety issues or newly identified risks?

* How are safety protections reevaluated after material model, product, or configuration changes?

### **Security & Resilliency**

#### **Definition**

Security & Resiliency refers to whether an AI system and its supporting infrastructure can protect against unauthorized access, manipulation, disruption, and other security threats while maintaining reliable operation and recovering from incidents.

For K–12 procurement, districts should assess whether the system has appropriate access controls, whether the specific product and its supporting components have been tested for relevant security vulnerabilities, and whether the vendor can detect, respond to, and recover from security incidents or service disruptions. Security evaluation should include both conventional cybersecurity risks and AI-specific threats, such as prompt injection or jailbreaks, where applicable. Districts should also determine whether the vendor will communicate significant vulnerabilities, incidents, and service disruptions within clearly defined timeframes.

**Example:** A district is considering an AI tutoring platform that stores student interactions and connects to district identity systems. The vendor provides a SOC 2 report and describes its organization-wide cybersecurity program but provides limited information about testing of the specific AI product.

The district therefore needs evidence about the security controls and testing applied to the product it would actually procure, including access permissions, vulnerabilities in the AI system, AI-specific attacks, incident response, and recovery capabilities. A general corporate security certification would not, by itself, establish that the specific product and its AI components are adequately secured.

#### **Checklist Requirements**

1) *The system uses appropriate authentication and access controls to prevent unauthorized users from accessing the system or its data.*

**What this means:** The district should determine how users authenticate and how the system prevents unauthorized access to the product, administrative functions, and associated data. Controls should be appropriate to the sensitivity of the system and the users who access it.

2) *Access to student, educator, administrative, and other sensitive information is limited according to user roles and permissions.*

**What this means:** Users should only be able to access information and system functions necessary for their roles. The district should determine whether the product supports appropriate role-based permissions and whether access to sensitive information or administrative functions is appropriately restricted.

3) *The vendor regularly assesses the system for security vulnerabilities and documents how identified vulnerabilities are addressed.*

**What this means:** Security should be evaluated through systematic vulnerability assessment rather than relying only on general security policies. The district should determine what testing is conducted, how frequently it occurs, what components are included, and how identified vulnerabilities are prioritized and remediated.

4) *The vendor tests the system against relevant attacks or manipulation techniques, including prompt injection, jailbreaks, or other adversarial inputs where applicable.*

**What this means:** AI systems can introduce security risks that conventional application-security testing may not identify. Where relevant to the system architecture and intended use, the district should determine whether the vendor has tested for AI-specific attacks and how identified vulnerabilities were addressed.

5) *The vendor has safeguards to detect and respond to unauthorized access, manipulation, or other security incidents.*

**What this means:** The district should determine whether the vendor can detect relevant security events and take appropriate action to contain and address them. This may include monitoring, alerting, access revocation, containment procedures, and other technical or operational controls.

6) *The vendor has a documented incident response process for security breaches or other significant system compromises.*

**What this means:** The vendor should have a defined process for detecting, containing, investigating, remediating, and recovering from security incidents. The district should also understand the respective responsibilities of the vendor and district during an incident.

7) *The system has appropriate continuity, backup, and recovery measures to maintain or restore essential functionality following failures or disruptions.*

**What this means:** Security and resiliency include the ability to continue or restore essential services after an outage, system failure, cyberattack, or other disruption. The district should determine what backup and recovery measures exist, how they are tested, and what limitations could affect service restoration.

8) *The vendor communicates significant security vulnerabilities, incidents, or service disruptions to the district within defined timeframes.*

**What this means:** Districts need timely information to assess and respond to security risks affecting their users or data. The district should determine what events trigger notification, how quickly the vendor will communicate them, what information will be provided, and whether notification obligations are contractually defined.

#### **Evidence to Obtain**

To evaluate the requirements above, the district should seek information demonstrating that the specific product, its supporting infrastructure, and relevant AI components are appropriately protected and that the vendor can respond to and recover from security events. Relevant evidence may include:

* **Authentication and access controls:** Documentation of authentication mechanisms, authorization controls, multi-factor authentication where appropriate, role-based access controls, administrator permissions, session controls, and other mechanisms used to prevent unauthorized access.

* **Role and permission structure:** Documentation showing which users and administrators can access student, educator, administrative, or other sensitive information and system functions. Districts should determine whether permissions follow the principle of limiting access to what is necessary for each role.

* **Security vulnerability assessments:** Results or summaries of vulnerability scanning, penetration testing, security assessments, or other testing conducted on the product and relevant infrastructure. Evidence should identify the scope, date, methodology, material findings, and remediation status where available.

* **Independent security assessments:** Relevant independent assessments or certifications, such as SOC 2 or ISO/IEC 27001, penetration-testing reports, or other qualified third-party assessments. Districts should examine the scope and date of an assessment rather than treating a certification as evidence that every component of the product is secure.

* **AI-specific security testing:** Results from testing for prompt injection, jailbreaks, adversarial inputs, data exfiltration, model manipulation, or other AI-specific threats relevant to the product. Evidence should identify which threats were tested, the testing methodology, vulnerabilities identified, and mitigations implemented.

* **Security failure and vulnerability history:** Documentation of material vulnerabilities or security weaknesses identified in the product, including severity, affected components, remediation status, and whether similar vulnerabilities remain unresolved.

* **Security monitoring and detection:** Information about monitoring, logging, alerting, anomaly detection, intrusion detection, or other controls used to identify unauthorized access, manipulation, or other security events.

* **Incident response procedures:** Documentation describing how security incidents are detected, contained, investigated, escalated, remediated, and recovered from, including vendor and district responsibilities.

* **Incident response testing:** Evidence that incident response procedures have been exercised or tested, such as tabletop exercises, simulations, or other response testing, where applicable.

* **Continuity and recovery measures:** Business continuity plans, disaster recovery procedures, backup mechanisms, service restoration procedures, and recovery objectives where available. Districts should determine what essential functionality can be restored and what dependencies or limitations could affect recovery.

* **Recovery testing:** Evidence that backup, restoration, or disaster recovery procedures have been tested and that material findings from those tests have been addressed.

* **Security incident notification procedures:** Documentation specifying what events trigger district notification, the required notification timeframe, communication channels, and information provided to affected districts.

* **Contractual security commitments:** Contract provisions, service-level agreements, or other commitments establishing responsibilities for security incidents, vulnerabilities, service disruptions, notification, and remediation.

If relevant information is unavailable, the district should seek additional documentation, clarification, testing, or validation before determining whether the applicable requirements are satisfied.

#### **Questions to Ask Vendors**

The following questions can help the district obtain and interpret evidence relevant to this dimension:

**Access Controls and Permissions**

* How are users authenticated and authorized to access the system?

* What authentication and access controls are available for students, educators, administrators, and other users?

* What roles and permissions are available, and how is access to sensitive data and administrative functions restricted?

* How are privileged accounts and administrative access protected?

* Can the district configure or restrict permissions based on user role?

**Security Testing and Vulnerabilities**

* What security assessments, vulnerability testing, or penetration testing have been conducted on the product and its supporting infrastructure?

* What components were included in the testing, when was it conducted, and what were the most significant findings?

* What vulnerabilities have been identified, and how were they prioritized and addressed?

* What independent security assessments or certifications are available, and what products, systems, and time periods do they cover?

* How frequently is the product reassessed for security vulnerabilities?

**AI-Specific Security**

* What AI-specific security threats have you tested for, including prompt injection, jailbreaks, adversarial inputs, or data exfiltration where applicable?

* What vulnerabilities or successful attacks were identified during this testing?

* What mitigations were implemented in response to identified vulnerabilities?

* How do you determine which AI-specific threats are relevant to this product and its intended use?

**Incident Response and Recovery**

* How are unauthorized access, manipulation, or other security incidents detected and investigated?

* What is the process for containing, remediating, and recovering from a security incident?

* What responsibilities would the vendor and district each have during a security incident?

* How often are incident response procedures tested or exercised?

* What backup, business continuity, and disaster recovery measures are in place?

* How frequently are recovery procedures tested, and what recovery objectives or service-restoration commitments apply?

**Notification and Ongoing Security**

* What events trigger notification to the district about a security vulnerability, incident, or service disruption?

* How quickly will the district be notified, and what information will the notification include?

* Are security incident and vulnerability notification obligations included in the contract?

* How does the vendor monitor for newly identified vulnerabilities or changes in the system's security posture?

* How are security controls and testing reevaluated after material changes to the product, infrastructure, or underlying AI components?

### **Privacy & Data Governance**

#### **Definition**

Privacy & Data Governance refers to whether student and educator data are collected, used, stored, shared, retained, and deleted in accordance with applicable legal, regulatory, contractual, and district requirements.

Districts should evaluate the full lifecycle of data used by the system, including what information is collected, why it is needed, how it is used, where it is stored, who receives it, how long it is retained, and how it is deleted. Privacy evaluation should address not only legal compliance, but also whether the system collects or uses more data than is necessary for the intended educational purpose.

Districts should pay particular attention to data used for model training or product improvement, third-party and subprocessor access, optional features such as memory or personalization, and whether material privacy commitments are reflected in enforceable contractual provisions.

**Example**: A district is considering an AI tutoring platform that collects student prompts, generated responses, usage information, and account information. The vendor's privacy policy states that data are protected and that the product complies with applicable privacy laws, but does not clearly explain whether student interactions are retained, shared with subprocessors, or used to improve the vendor's models.

Before adoption, the district would need to establish what data the system collects, whether each category is necessary for the intended tutoring function, how the data move through the system, whether student data are used for model training or product improvement, how long data are retained, and whether the district can require deletion. A general privacy policy or compliance statement alone would not establish that these requirements are satisfied.

#### **Checklist Requirements**

1) *The vendor clearly identifies what student and educator data are collected by the system.*

**What this means in practice:** The district should be able to identify the categories of data the product collects, generates, receives, or otherwise processes. This may include prompts, outputs, student records, account information, usage data, metadata, audio or visual information, or other information depending on the product. The description should be specific enough for the district to determine whether the collection is appropriate for the intended use.

Privacy commitments should not rely solely on public-facing policies or vendor assurances when the protection is material to the district's decision. Districts should determine whether key data-use restrictions can be incorporated into enforceable contractual terms.

2) *The vendor collects only the data reasonably necessary to provide the intended service.*

**What this means in practice:** The district should consider whether each category of collected data serves a legitimate function required for the intended educational use. Optional features such as persistent memory, personalization, behavioral tracking, or additional analytics may introduce data practices that are not necessary to provide the core service.

The question is not simply whether collection is permitted, but whether the amount and type of data collected are proportionate to what the product is being used to accomplish.

3) *The vendor clearly documents how student and educator data are used, including whether data are used for model training or product improvement.*

**What this means in practice:** The district should be able to distinguish among uses such as providing the contracted service, troubleshooting, safety monitoring, analytics, product improvement, evaluation, model training, or other purposes. Statements should identify whether data are used directly by the vendor, by underlying model providers, or by third parties.

4) *Student data are not used to train the vendor's foundational models unless the district has explicitly authorized such use and it is legally and contractually permitted.*

**What this means in practice:** The district should establish whether student data are used to train, fine-tune, evaluate, or otherwise improve foundational or general-purpose models. If such use is proposed, the district should determine whether it has explicitly authorized the practice and whether the use is permitted under applicable law and the district's agreement with the vendor.

5) *The vendor provides a clear data-flow description or diagram showing how data move through the system and to third parties or subprocessors.*

**What this means in practice:** The district should be able to trace relevant data through the product, including where data enter the system, what services or components process them, which third parties or subprocessors receive them, and where they are stored or transferred. The purpose is to make material data-sharing and processing relationships visible rather than relying solely on a general privacy statement.

6) *The vendor clearly documents where and for how long student and educator data are stored and retained.*

**What this means in practice:** The district should understand the relevant storage locations or regions, retention periods, and whether different categories of data are retained for different lengths of time. Retention should be considered across active systems, backups, and other relevant storage environments where applicable.

7) *The vendor provides a mechanism for authorized district data to be deleted when retention is no longer necessary or when required by law or contract.*

**What this means in practice:** The district should know how authorized deletion requests are made, what systems or data they cover, and how the vendor handles deletion from relevant systems and backups where applicable. The district should also understand any documented exceptions, such as legal or other authorized retention requirements.

8) *Student and educator data are protected through appropriate encryption, access controls, and other security measures.*

**What this means in practice:** The district should establish that data are protected against unauthorized access or disclosure through technical and organizational controls appropriate to the sensitivity and use of the information. Relevant controls may include encryption in transit and at rest, role-based access controls, privileged-access restrictions, authentication requirements, and other safeguards.

9) *Third-party sharing and subprocessor practices are clearly documented and consistent with district requirements.*

**What this means in practice:** The district should know which third parties or subprocessors receive or process district data, what each party does, what categories of data are involved, and where relevant data are transferred. These practices should be consistent with the district's privacy requirements and contractual commitments.

10) *The vendor's data practices comply with applicable legal, regulatory, contractual, and district requirements.*

**What this means in practice:** The district should determine which privacy requirements apply to the intended use and whether the vendor can demonstrate compliance with them. This may include FERPA, COPPA where applicable, state student-privacy requirements, and district-specific contractual requirements. Compliance should be reflected in the relevant contractual relationship rather than relying solely on a vendor's general statement of compliance.

#### **Evidence to Obtain**

* **Data inventory:** Categories of student and educator data collected, generated, received, or processed by the product.

* **Data-collection specifications:** Identification of required versus optional data fields and features.

* **Purpose-of-use documentation:** Documentation distinguishing service delivery, analytics, troubleshooting, safety, evaluation, product improvement, model training, and other uses.

* **Model-training and improvement policies:** Documentation stating whether student or educator data are used for training, fine-tuning, evaluation, or improvement of foundational or other models.

* **Contractual data-use restrictions:** Data-processing agreements, privacy agreements, terms of service, and other enforceable commitments governing permitted and prohibited uses.

* **Data-flow diagrams:** Diagrams showing how data move through the product, including relevant systems, model providers, third parties, and subprocessors.

* **Subprocessor documentation:** Current subprocessor lists, purposes of processing, categories of data shared, locations, and applicable notice or approval processes.

* **Storage and transfer information:** Storage locations or regions, data-transfer practices, and relevant hosting arrangements.

* **Retention schedules:** Retention periods by relevant data category, including applicable backup or archival practices.

* **Deletion procedures:** Documentation describing deletion requests, deletion mechanisms, scope of deletion, timing, and treatment of backups where applicable.

* **Technical privacy and security controls:** Encryption, access controls, authentication, role-based permissions, and other controls protecting student and educator data.

* **Privacy compliance documentation:** FERPA/COPPA documentation where applicable, state student-privacy compliance materials, privacy assessments, or other relevant compliance records.

* **District contractual requirements:** Completed or proposed district data privacy agreement and identification of any provisions the vendor cannot meet.

* **Privacy incident history:** Material privacy incidents, affected data or users, response actions, and corrective measures where relevant.

* **Evidence of implementation:** Technical or operational documentation supporting that stated privacy commitments are actually implemented, rather than relying solely on policy language.

#### **Questions to Ask Vendors**

#### **Data Collection and Minimization**

* What categories of student and educator data does the product collect, generate, receive, or process?

* Which data elements are required to provide the intended service, and which are optional?

* Why is each category of data necessary for the intended educational use?

* Does the product collect any data for purposes unrelated to providing the contracted service?

* Do features such as memory, personalization, analytics, or behavioral tracking introduce additional data collection?

#### **Data Use and Model Training**

* How is each category of student and educator data used?

* Are student or educator prompts, inputs, outputs, or other data used for model training, fine-tuning, evaluation, or product improvement?

* Are data shared with an underlying model provider for any of these purposes?

* Are any data used for purposes beyond delivering the contracted service, and are those uses reflected in the district's agreement?

* If student data are used for model training or improvement, what authorization permits that use?

#### **Data Flows, Third Parties, and Subprocessors**

* Which third parties or subprocessors receive or process district data?

* What purpose does each third party or subprocessor serve?

* What categories of data are shared with each party?

* Where does relevant data move within the system?

* Where are data stored and processed?

* How are districts notified when subprocessors or material data-processing practices change?

#### **Retention and Deletion**

* How long is each category of student and educator data retained?

* Are different retention periods applied to prompts, outputs, account information, logs, or other data?

* What happens to data in backups when the primary data are deleted?

* How can the district request deletion?

* How long does deletion take, and what evidence can the vendor provide that deletion occurred?

* Are there circumstances in which data cannot be deleted, and what are those circumstances?

#### **Privacy Controls and Compliance**

* What encryption and access controls protect student and educator data?

* Which personnel or systems can access student data, and how is that access restricted?

* What applicable federal and state privacy requirements govern the product?

* How does the vendor demonstrate compliance with those requirements?

* Will the vendor sign the district's data privacy agreement?

* Are any vendor terms or data practices inconsistent with the district's privacy requirements?

#### **Privacy Incidents and Ongoing Changes**

* Have there been material privacy incidents involving the product?

* What occurred, what data were affected, and how did the vendor respond?

* What corrective actions were taken?

* How does the vendor monitor changes to its data practices, subprocessors, models, or infrastructure?

* What privacy-related changes would trigger reevaluation or notification to the district?

### **Accountability & Transparency**

#### **Definition**

Accountability & Transparency refers to whether an AI system’s capabilities, limitations, responsibilities, and material risks are sufficiently documented and whether appropriate mechanisms exist for oversight and review.

For K–12 procurement, districts should be able to understand how the system is intended to be used, what limitations and known failure modes it has, who is responsible when problems occur, and what information the district can access to evaluate and oversee the system. Transparency should be specific to the product being evaluated and sufficiently current to reflect its actual configuration and use.

**Example**: A district is evaluating an AI tutoring platform that the vendor demonstrates using one underlying model but deploys using a different or subsequently updated configuration. The vendor provides general documentation about responsible AI but does not clearly describe the production system, its known limitations, or when material changes will occur. If the district cannot determine which system it is actually evaluating or who is responsible when problems occur, it cannot meaningfully oversee the technology or interpret evidence from the evaluation.

#### **Checklist Requirements**

1) *The vendor clearly identifies the model, system, or AI technology powering the relevant student or educator experience, including version information where appropriate.*

**What this means:** The district should be able to identify what AI technology it is actually evaluating, including the relevant model, system, version, or configuration when those details affect performance or oversight. This helps the district distinguish the evaluated system from a different model or configuration that may later be deployed.

2) *The vendor clearly documents the system’s intended uses, limitations, and known failure modes relevant to the district’s use case.*

**What this means:** Documentation should explain what the system is designed to do, what uses are unsupported or inappropriate, and where the system may perform unreliably or fail. Districts should look for product-specific information rather than relying solely on general corporate responsible-AI policies.

3) *The vendor provides sufficient information about how the system behaves to enable meaningful district oversight.*

**What this means:** Districts should have enough visibility into system behavior to evaluate performance, investigate concerns, and oversee whether the system continues to operate as expected. The information provided should be sufficient for the district to identify and investigate material issues rather than requiring complete reliance on vendor claims.

4) *The system used during demonstrations or evaluations is representative of the production system, or material differences are clearly disclosed.*

**What this means:** Evidence gathered during procurement should correspond to the system the district would actually receive. If the demonstration or evaluation uses a different model, configuration, retrieval system, functionality, or other material component, those differences should be disclosed so the district can determine whether the evidence remains relevant.

5) *The vendor provides advance notice of significant model, system, or functionality changes that could affect performance, safety, privacy, or other evaluation criteria.*

**What this means:** Districts should know when material changes could alter the characteristics they evaluated during procurement. Advance notice allows the district to determine whether additional testing, review, contractual action, or reevaluation is necessary.

6) *The district can determine which model or system version produced an output when versioning is relevant to oversight or incident investigation.*

**What this means:** When different versions can produce materially different outputs, the district should be able to identify the relevant version when reviewing an output, investigating an incident, or evaluating a change in performance. This creates traceability between system behavior and the technology that produced it.

7) *The vendor clearly identifies roles and responsibilities for addressing system errors, harmful outputs, privacy incidents, and other significant failures.*

**What this means:** The district should understand which responsibilities belong to the vendor and which remain with the district when errors, harmful outputs, privacy incidents, security incidents, or other material failures occur. Responsibilities should be specific enough to guide action when a problem arises.

8) *The vendor provides a documented process for reporting concerns and obtaining remediation or support when significant problems occur.*

**What this means:** The district should know how to report significant concerns, who receives those reports, how issues are investigated, and what remediation or support the vendor provides. The process should give the district a defined mechanism for escalating material problems rather than relying on informal vendor contacts.

9) *Relevant system changes, incidents, and evaluation information are documented sufficiently to support district audit and oversight.*

**What this means:** The vendor should maintain sufficient documentation for the district to understand material changes, significant incidents, and relevant evaluation findings over time. Where appropriate, districts should also have access to relevant logs, evaluation data, audit provisions, or independent review mechanisms.

#### **Evidence to Obtain**

* **System and model documentation:** Product documentation, model or system identification, version information, system architecture or configuration information where relevant, and the Vendor AI Model Disclosure Form.

* **Intended-use and limitation documentation:** Intended and prohibited uses, known limitations, known failure modes, unsupported use cases, and technical or governance documentation.

* **System behavior information:** Documentation describing relevant system behavior, operating boundaries, monitoring practices, and information available to support district oversight.

* **Evaluation-to-production correspondence:** Documentation identifying the model, version, configuration, prompts, retrieval components, or other material system characteristics used during demonstrations and evaluations, along with documentation of differences from production.

* **Change-management documentation:** Model and system change policies, version histories, change logs, advance-notification procedures, and criteria for determining when a change requires reevaluation.

* **Output traceability:** Version identifiers, timestamps, logs, or other records that allow relevant outputs to be associated with the system version that produced them.

* **Roles and responsibilities:** Contracts, data privacy agreements, terms of service, responsibility matrices, incident-response procedures, and other documentation specifying vendor and district responsibilities.

* **Incident history and response:** Material safety, security, privacy, or performance incidents; incident records or summaries; investigation findings; corrective actions; and remediation documentation.

* **Oversight and audit access:** Log-access capabilities, evaluation datasets or reports, audit provisions, independent-review provisions, contractual access rights, and documentation of legitimate proprietary or privacy limitations and available alternatives.

* **Ongoing documentation:** Records showing how system changes, incidents, evaluations, and other material information are documented and maintained over time.

* **Contractual commitments:** Data privacy agreements, terms of service, procurement agreements, service-level commitments, change-notification provisions, audit rights, incident-notification requirements, and other enforceable provisions relevant to the Requirements being evaluated.

#### **Questions to Ask Vendors**

**System Identity and Documentation**

* What model, system, or AI technology powers the relevant student or educator experience?

* What version or configuration is currently being evaluated and would be deployed?

* What are the system’s intended uses and prohibited or unsupported uses?

* How is this documentation kept current as the system changes?

**Evaluation and Production System**

* Is the system used in this demonstration or evaluation the same model, version, and configuration that would be deployed?

* How are material system changes communicated to districts before deployment?

**Oversight and Traceability**

* What information can the district access to understand and oversee system behavior?

* What logs or evaluation data can the district access?

* Can the district determine which model or system version produced a particular output?

* What information is available to support investigation of a significant incident or unexpected change in performance?

* Can the district conduct or commission an independent review when appropriate?

* If direct access is limited by proprietary, privacy, or security constraints, what alternative evidence is available?

* What responsibilities does the vendor assume for system errors and harmful outputs?

### **Explainability & Interpretability**

#### **Definition**

Explainability & Interpretability refers to whether an AI system provides users with understandable information about how its outputs or recommendations were produced and what limitations may affect their interpretation. For K–12 procurement, this Dimension asks whether educators can access enough information to understand AI outputs and recommendations, including relevant sources, inputs, or factors, and whether they can recognize situations in which an explanation may be incomplete, misleading, or unavailable.

**Example**: A district is evaluating an AI system that recommends instructional interventions for individual students. The system provides a brief explanation alongside each recommendation, but the explanation does not identify which student information or factors informed the recommendation, and the vendor does not disclose when explanations may be incomplete or unreliable. Although the system appears transparent, educators may not have enough information to determine whether a recommendation is appropriate for a particular student or when the explanation itself should be treated cautiously.

#### **Checklist Requirements**

1) *The system provides explanations of outputs or recommendations that are understandable and useful to educators and administrators.*

**What this means:** The explanation should help educators make sense of an AI-generated output or recommendation without requiring specialized technical expertise. Districts should assess whether the information provided is understandable, relevant to the user’s role, and useful for deciding whether and how to use the output.

2) *Educators can identify the relevant information, inputs, or factors that informed an AI-generated recommendation, classification, or decision.*

**What this means:** Where relevant to the use case, educators should be able to identify the student information, sources, inputs, or other factors that contributed to an output or recommendation. Technical descriptions of how a model works are not necessarily sufficient if they do not help educators understand why a particular output was produced.

3) *Explanations provide sufficient context for educators to evaluate whether an output is appropriate for the student or instructional situation.*

**What this means:** Explanations should provide information that helps educators judge whether an output makes sense in the context in which they intend to use it. The relevant information will depend on the educational task, but should be sufficient to support informed interpretation rather than simply describing the system technically.

4) *The system communicates relevant limitations, uncertainty, or confidence information when needed to interpret an output appropriately.*

**What this means in practice:** When uncertainty or limitations could affect how an output should be interpreted, the system should communicate that information in a form educators can understand and use. Confidence indicators, uncertainty information, or other contextual signals may be appropriate depending on the task.

5) *The vendor documents known situations in which the system’s explanations or outputs may be incomplete, misleading, or unreliable.*

**What this means:** Districts should understand when explanations may not provide a complete or accurate account of how an output was produced and when outputs themselves may be unreliable. Clear explanations should not create false confidence that the explanation fully represents the system’s underlying process.

6) *The level of explanation provided is appropriate to the potential consequences of the AI-supported decision or recommendation.*

**What this means:** The amount and type of explanation should be appropriate to the importance and potential consequences of the decision or recommendation. Higher-consequence uses may require more information for educators to understand, question, and appropriately interpret an AI output.

#### **Evidence to Obtain**

* **Explanation features and examples:** Product demonstrations, screenshots or recordings of explanation features, user interfaces, product documentation, and representative examples showing the information educators receive alongside outputs or recommendations.

* **Sources, inputs, and contributing factors:** Documentation or product functionality showing what information, sources, inputs, or factors can be identified as contributing to an AI-generated recommendation, classification, or decision.

* **User-centered explanation testing:** Evidence that educators or other intended users can understand and appropriately interpret the explanations, including usability testing, comprehension testing, user studies, or other evaluations of explanation quality.

* **Explanation usefulness and relevance:** Evidence showing whether explanations help users determine whether an output is appropriate for the intended student, instructional situation, or educational task rather than simply providing technical information.

* **Uncertainty and confidence information:** Documentation of confidence scores, uncertainty indicators, source attribution, or other information provided to help users interpret outputs where relevant to the use case.

* **Explanation limitations:** Vendor documentation identifying circumstances in which explanations may be incomplete, unavailable, misleading, or otherwise unreliable, including known limitations of the explanation method used.

* **Explanation reliability testing:** Testing or validation examining whether explanations accurately and consistently represent the relevant factors associated with system outputs and identifying situations where they may not.

* **Examples of explanation failures:** Documented examples or test results showing incomplete, misleading, inconsistent, or otherwise unreliable explanations and how those limitations are communicated to users.

* **Task- and consequence-appropriate explanation design:** Evidence that the level and type of explanation provided were designed or evaluated in relation to the role of the user and the potential consequences of the AI-supported decision or recommendation.

* **Technical and product documentation:** Documentation explaining how the system generates, selects, or presents explanations and any assumptions or limitations that affect their interpretation.

#### **Questions to Ask Vendors**

**Educator Understanding and Use**

* What information can educators access to understand an AI output or recommendation?

* Does the system provide plain-language explanations?

* How has the vendor evaluated whether educators understand and can use the explanations?

* Can educators determine whether an explanation is relevant to the instructional decision they are making?

* What information is available without requiring specialized technical expertise?

**Sources, Inputs, and Contributing Factors**

* Can educators see the relevant sources, inputs, or factors that informed a recommendation or decision?

* What student or instructional information is considered when generating the output?

* Which factors can the system identify as contributing to a particular recommendation?

* Are there situations in which relevant inputs or contributing factors cannot be shown to educators?

**Uncertainty and Limitations**

* Does the system provide confidence, uncertainty, or other indicators that help educators interpret outputs?

* When are those indicators provided, and how should educators interpret them?

* When might an explanation be unavailable or incomplete?

* Are there circumstances in which an explanation may not accurately reflect the factors that produced an output?

* What known limitations or failure modes affect the system’s explanations?

* How are those limitations communicated to educators and districts?

**Testing and Validation**

* How has the vendor evaluated the reliability of its explanation features?

* Has the vendor tested whether explanations are understandable to educators using the product?

* Has the vendor tested whether explanations accurately reflect the factors associated with system outputs?

* What examples of incomplete, misleading, or unreliable explanations have been identified?

* Has an independent party evaluated the system’s explanation capabilities?

**Decision Context and Consequences**

* How does the level of explanation provided vary based on the type or consequence of the AI-supported decision?

* What additional information is available for higher-consequence recommendations or decisions?

* How does the vendor determine what information educators need to appropriately question or act on an AI output?

### **Fairness & Bias Mitigation**

#### **Definition**

Fairness & Bias Mitigation refers to whether an AI system performs equitably across relevant student populations and is evaluated for disparities or biases that could introduce, reinforce, or amplify existing inequities.

For K–12 procurement, this Dimension asks whether the system has been evaluated across student populations relevant to the district’s intended use, whether disparities in performance or outcomes have been identified and addressed, and whether the system provides appropriate support for multilingual learners and students with disabilities.

**Example:** A district is evaluating an AI system that recommends instructional interventions for students. The vendor reports strong overall performance but does not provide subgroup results or explain whether the system was tested with English learners or students with disabilities. The district therefore cannot determine whether the system performs equitably for the populations it serves, even if its aggregate performance appears strong.

#### **Checklist Requirements**

1) *The vendor has evaluated system performance across student populations relevant to the district’s intended use.*

**What this means:** The district should determine whether the vendor evaluated performance across populations relevant to the specific educational use rather than relying only on aggregate results. Relevant populations may include race or ethnicity, English learner status, disability, gender, income, grade level, or other characteristics that could affect system performance in the intended context.

2) *The vendor has tested for disparities in accuracy, error rates, recommendations, classifications, or other relevant outcomes across relevant student populations.*

**What this means**: Fairness evaluation should examine whether the system produces meaningfully different outcomes across populations. The relevant outcomes will depend on the use case and may include differences in accuracy, false-positive or false-negative rates, recommendations, classifications, or other measures that could affect students differently.

3) *The vendor evaluates potential differences associated with relevant characteristics such as race/ethnicity, English learner status, disability, age, or other contextually relevant factors.*

**What this means**: Districts should assess whether the vendor considered characteristics that are relevant to the students and educational context in which the system will be used. The relevant characteristics will vary by use case, and vendors should be able to explain why particular populations or characteristics were included or excluded from evaluation.

4) *The vendor can explain how potential sources of bias are identified, evaluated, and mitigated.*

**What this means**: The vendor should be able to describe how it identifies potential sources of bias, how those risks are evaluated, and what steps are taken when disparities or other concerns are identified. A statement that the system is “bias tested” is not sufficient without information about the methods used and how findings affected the system.

5) *The vendor documents identified disparities or limitations and the steps taken to address them.*

**What this means**: Districts should understand what material disparities or limitations have been identified, which populations or outcomes they affect, and what remediation or mitigation efforts have been undertaken. Known limitations should not be omitted simply because they have not been fully resolved.

6) *The system has been evaluated for its potential to introduce, reinforce, or amplify existing inequities in the intended educational context.*

**What this means**: Fairness evaluation should consider more than whether performance is statistically similar across groups. Districts should also consider whether the way the system is designed or used could create, reinforce, or amplify existing educational inequities in the specific context of deployment.

7) *Fairness evaluations are based on evidence from populations and use cases that are relevant to the district’s intended deployment.*

**What this means**: Results from a different population, educational setting, task, or system configuration may not establish equitable performance in the district’s context. Districts should examine whether the populations, tasks, data, and conditions used in evaluation are sufficiently representative of the intended deployment.

#### **Evidence to Obtain**

* **Subgroup performance results:** Accuracy, precision, recall, F1, false-positive and false-negative rates, recommendation distributions, classification outcomes, or other task-appropriate measures reported across relevant student populations.

* **Disparity analyses:** Comparisons of outcomes across relevant populations, including the statistical methods used to identify meaningful differences.

* **Evaluation population characteristics:** Sample sizes, demographic composition, grade levels, disability status, English learner status, socioeconomic characteristics, and other relevant characteristics of evaluation populations.

* **Evaluation methodology:** Testing protocols, evaluation tasks, data sources, inclusion and exclusion criteria, sampling methods, and whether evaluation data were representative or held out from system development.

* **Use-case-specific testing:** Evidence from tasks, subjects, grade levels, settings, and workflows that correspond to the district’s intended use.

* **Error and failure analysis:** Differences in error types or failure modes across relevant populations, including examples of systematic or consequential errors.

* **Bias and fairness testing methodology:** Methods used to identify potential sources of bias and evaluate disparate outcomes.

* **Identified disparities and limitations:** Documentation of known disparities, affected populations, relevant system limitations, and circumstances in which equitable performance has not been established.

* **Mitigation measures:** Changes to data, models, prompts, algorithms, product design, safeguards, or workflows made in response to identified fairness concerns.

* **Post-mitigation results:** Comparative testing showing whether mitigation efforts reduced identified disparities or otherwise improved fairness-related outcomes.

* **Ongoing monitoring:** Metrics, monitoring procedures, reevaluation schedules, and triggers used to identify emerging or changing disparities after system updates or changes in use.

* **Language and accessibility evidence:** Documentation and testing related to multilingual support, accessibility features, assistive technology compatibility, and performance with relevant student populations.

* **Independent evaluation:** Third-party or independently conducted fairness evaluations, where available, including information about evaluator independence, methodology, populations tested, and findings.

* **System-version correspondence:** Evidence that fairness testing applies to the model, product version, configuration, and relevant system components being evaluated by the district.

#### **Questions to Ask Vendors**

**Population Coverage and Evaluation**

* Which student populations were included in performance and fairness evaluations?

* How were those populations represented in the evaluation data?

* Which populations or characteristics were not evaluated, and why?

* Were the populations, tasks, and conditions representative of the district’s intended use?

* Can you provide subgroup-level performance results rather than only aggregate results?

**Performance and Disparities**

* Which outcomes were compared across student populations?

* Were differences in accuracy, error rates, recommendations, classifications, or other relevant outcomes identified?

* What disparities have been identified across relevant populations?

* What statistical or other methods were used to determine whether differences were meaningful?

* Are there known populations for which system performance is weaker?

**Bias Identification and Mitigation**

* How does the vendor identify potential sources of bias?

* What types of bias or fairness risks have been evaluated?

* What disparities or limitations have been identified?

* What steps were taken to address identified disparities or sources of bias?

* How did the vendor evaluate whether those mitigation efforts were effective?

* Are there known fairness limitations that remain unresolved?

**Intended Context and Equity**

* How was the system evaluated for the potential to introduce, reinforce, or amplify existing inequities?

* Were evaluations conducted in educational settings and tasks similar to the district’s intended deployment?

* Are there circumstances in which the system may perform equitably on average but create different risks or barriers for particular students?

* How does the system support multilingual learners and students with disabilities?

* Which assistive technologies and accessibility features have been tested with relevant users?

**Ongoing Monitoring**

* How does the vendor monitor fairness-related performance after deployment?

* Which metrics or indicators are monitored?

* How frequently are fairness evaluations repeated?

* What triggers reevaluation after a model, data, or product change?

* How are newly identified disparities communicated to districts?

### **Human Oversight & Agency**

#### **Definition**

Human Oversight & Agency refers to whether educators and administrators retain meaningful control over AI-supported decisions and can review, question, modify, override, or reject AI outputs when appropriate.

For K–12 procurement, this Dimension asks whether appropriate human review occurs before consequential AI-supported decisions, whether educators can meaningfully exercise judgment over AI outputs, and whether the district can establish and enforce boundaries around how the system is used.

Where AI is used to support consequential educational decisions, districts should determine not only whether human oversight is technically available but whether the vendor contract, product configuration, and operating procedures preserve the district's authority to exercise that oversight.

**Example:** A district is evaluating an AI system that recommends interventions for individual students. Educators technically have the ability to override recommendations, but the system provides little information about why a recommendation was made and requires several steps or administrative permissions to reject it. The district should not treat the existence of an “override” button as sufficient evidence of meaningful human control.

#### **Checklist Requirements**

1) *The system provides appropriate human review before AI outputs are used to make high-stakes decisions.*

**What this means**: AI outputs should not independently determine high-stakes educational decisions when human judgment is necessary. The district should establish where human review is required based on the potential consequences of the decision.

2) *Educators and administrators can review AI-generated outputs before acting on them when human judgment is necessary.*

**What this means**: Users should have a meaningful opportunity to examine an AI output before it is communicated to students, incorporated into a decision, or otherwise acted upon when human judgment is necessary. A nominal human role is not sufficient if users are expected to accept outputs without meaningful review.

3) *Educators can question, override, modify, or reject AI recommendations when appropriate.*

**What this means**: Human oversight is meaningful only when educators retain the ability to disagree with an AI recommendation and act accordingly. The degree of control should reflect the consequences of the AI-supported task, and authorized users should not be prevented from exercising appropriate judgment.

4) *Human overrides can be made without unreasonable technical or procedural barriers.*

**What this means**: An override that technically exists but is difficult, burdensome, or impractical to use may not provide meaningful human control. Districts should examine the steps, permissions, approvals, and workflow required for educators to reverse or modify an AI-generated output or action.

5) *The system clearly distinguishes AI-generated recommendations or outputs from decisions made by educators or administrators.*

**What this means**: Users should be able to distinguish between what the AI generated and what a human decided. Clear labeling and documentation of human actions help preserve accountability and prevent AI outputs from being mistaken for independent professional judgments.

6) *Educators receive guidance on appropriate and inappropriate uses of the system, including situations in which AI outputs should not be relied upon.*

**What this means**: Educators should receive usable guidance about the system’s intended uses, limitations, known failure modes, and circumstances requiring additional human judgment. Guidance should also be updated when material system capabilities or limitations change.

7) *Educators have sufficient information about an AI output to exercise meaningful judgment rather than simply accepting the system’s recommendation.*

**What this means**: Human review is not meaningful if educators lack the information needed to evaluate an output. Users should have enough relevant context, such as explanations, sources, inputs, or uncertainty information where appropriate, to determine whether an output is suitable for the student, task, or situation.

8) *The district can establish and enforce appropriate boundaries for when and how the AI system may be used.*

**What this means**: Districts should be able to define appropriate uses, restrict inappropriate uses, and configure or disable AI features when necessary. Where appropriate, these boundaries should be technically enforceable rather than relying solely on individual user discretion.

#### **Evidence to Obtain**

* **Human-review workflows:** Product workflows, demonstrations, documentation, and configuration settings showing when and how educators or administrators review AI outputs before consequential decisions or actions.

* **Review opportunities:** Evidence showing whether users can examine outputs before they are communicated, acted upon, or incorporated into decisions.

* **Override and modification controls:** Demonstrations, interface controls, configuration options, and workflow documentation showing whether authorized users can question, modify, override, or reject AI recommendations.

* **Override accessibility:** Number of steps required to override an output, required permissions or approvals, role restrictions, and usability evidence showing whether overrides can be exercised practically at the point of decision.

* **AI-versus-human decision labeling:** Interface examples, system documentation, labels or indicators identifying AI-generated content, and records showing how final human decisions are distinguished or recorded.

* **Appropriate-use guidance:** Training materials, user guidance, acceptable-use policies, documentation of inappropriate reliance, and information about known limitations or failure modes.

* **Meaningful decision-support information:** Output explanations, relevant inputs or sources, confidence or uncertainty information, and other contextual information available to educators when reviewing an output.

* **Human-oversight testing:** User testing or evaluation showing whether educators understand their role, can appropriately question outputs, and can exercise available controls.

* **District-level controls:** Administrative settings, role-based permissions, feature restrictions, disablement options, usage controls, and other mechanisms for establishing boundaries around AI use.

* **Enforceability of boundaries:** Evidence showing whether district restrictions can be technically enforced rather than relying exclusively on user compliance.

* **High-stakes use safeguards:** Documentation identifying which outputs or capabilities may affect high-stakes decisions and what human-review controls apply to those uses.

* **System limitations affecting agency:** Known circumstances in which users cannot review, modify, override, or otherwise control AI outputs, including technical or procedural constraints.

#### **Questions to Ask Vendors**

**Human Review and High-Stakes Decisions**

* Which outputs can be used in high-stakes educational decisions?

* Does the system require or support human review before those outputs are acted upon?

* Can the district configure when human review is required?

* Are there circumstances in which the system can make or trigger a consequential decision without human review?

* Can users examine an output before it is communicated to students or incorporated into a decision?

**User Control and Overrides**

* Can educators question or challenge an AI recommendation?

* Can they modify, override, or reject the recommendation?

* Are any outputs locked or automatically enforced?

* How many steps are required to override an AI output or recommendation?

* Are special permissions or administrative approval required?

* Can authorized users override the system at the point where the AI output is being used?

* Are there circumstances in which an override is technically available but operationally restricted?

**AI and Human Decision-Making**

* How are AI-generated outputs identified?

* Can users distinguish an AI recommendation from a human decision?

* Are AI-generated outputs clearly labeled when presented to educators, administrators, or students?

* How is the final human decision recorded when one is required?

**Guidance and Appropriate Use**

* What guidance is provided to educators about appropriate and inappropriate use?

* Are situations in which outputs should not be relied upon explicitly identified?

* Does the guidance address known limitations or failure modes?

* Is guidance updated when material system capabilities or limitations change?

**Information for Human Judgment**

* What information is available to educators when they review an output?

* Can educators understand relevant inputs, sources, or context?

* What information helps users determine whether an output should be accepted, modified, or rejected?

* Are there situations in which users receive an output without enough context to evaluate it?

**District Controls and Boundaries**

* What controls can the district use to configure or restrict AI features?

* Can the district disable particular features or uses?

* Can permissions be assigned by user or role?

* Can the district establish boundaries for which users, tasks, or contexts may use particular AI capabilities?

* Can those boundaries be enforced technically rather than relying solely on user discretion?

## **The Six-Step Evaluation Process In Detail**

The Six-Step Evaluation Process provides a consistent method for moving from an AI system's intended use to Requirement-level findings. Steps 1–3 establish the context and evaluation expectations for the procurement, while Steps 4–6 are applied to each relevant Requirement. The specific evidence, questions, and performance considerations will vary according to the AI system, educational use, and context.

The process should be treated as iterative rather than strictly linear. When evidence is incomplete or raises questions about performance, districts may return to evidence gathering, request additional information, conduct testing, or seek validation before making a final Requirement Determination.

### **Step 1\. Define the Use and Context**

Establish the educational purpose and context in which the AI system will be evaluated. The intended use, users, operating conditions, and potential consequences of failure provide the foundation for identifying relevant Requirements and determining appropriate evaluation expectations.

Before evaluating the vendor or system, establish:

* **Educational purpose:** The educational problem or need the AI is intended to address.

* **Intended use:** The specific functions the system will perform and the activities, decisions, or outputs it will support.

* **Intended users:** The educators, students, administrators, or other users who will interact with or rely on the system.

* **Affected populations:** The students, educators, or other groups who could be affected by the system's outputs or decisions.

* **Operating conditions:** Where, when, and under what conditions the system will be used.

* **Inputs and dependencies:** The information, data, tools, or other system components on which the AI will rely.

* **Consequences of failure:** The potential effects of inaccurate, harmful, biased, insecure, or otherwise inappropriate outputs.

* **Boundaries of use:** The uses for which the system will be permitted, restricted, or prohibited.

### **Step 2\. Identify Relevant Requirements**

Determine which Requirements should be evaluated based on the AI system’s intended use and context. The Eight Trustworthiness Dimensions provide the overall structure for evaluation, while the specific Requirements identify what should be true of the system for the particular educational use.

After defining the use and context, identify:

* **Relevant dimensions:** The Trustworthiness Dimensions that are applicable to the AI system and its intended use.

* **Applicable Requirements:** The specific Requirements within those dimensions that should be evaluated for the intended use, users, populations, and operating conditions.

* **Use-specific considerations:** Any characteristics of the AI system or educational context that make particular Requirements especially relevant.

* **Potential consequences:** Requirements that warrant particular attention because failure could result in significant educational, safety, privacy, equity, or other consequences.

* **Requirements to evaluate:** A documented set of relevant Requirements to carry forward into the remaining evaluation steps, including the rationale for excluding Requirements that are not applicable.

### **Step 3\. Establish Importance and Success**

Determine how important each relevant Requirement is to the evaluation and what would constitute adequate performance for the intended use. Establishing these expectations before reviewing vendor results helps ensure that available evidence does not determine what the district considers acceptable.

For each relevant Requirement, document:

* **Requirement Importance:** The importance of the Requirement based on its potential consequences and relevance to the intended use, using the levels Non-Negotiable, High Priority, Preferred, or Informational.

* **Performance Standard or Benchmark:** The level, condition, or outcome that would constitute adequate performance for the specific educational use and context.

* **Evaluation conditions:** The users, populations, tasks, operating conditions, or other circumstances that should be reflected when determining whether the standard has been met.

* **Evidence expectations:** The types of evidence that would allow the district to determine whether the Requirement meets its established standard.

* **Basis for the standard:** The relevant research, professional standards, regulatory or contractual requirements, validated benchmarks, or other appropriate sources used to establish the standard.

### **Step 4\. Gather Evidence**

Collect the information needed to evaluate whether each relevant Requirement is satisfied and whether the system meets the established Performance Standard or Benchmark. Evidence should be relevant to the specific AI system, intended use, users, populations, and operating conditions being evaluated.

For each relevant Requirement, gather:

* **Vendor evidence:** Documentation, policies, technical materials, performance results, testing records, or other information provided by the vendor.

* **Independent evidence:** External evaluations, audits, validation studies, research, certifications, or other credible sources that can substantiate vendor claims.

* **Testing and validation:** District testing, demonstrations, benchmark results, or other evaluations that provide evidence about system performance in the intended context.

* **Evidence scope:** The populations, tasks, conditions, system version, and time period covered by each piece of evidence.

* **Evidence limitations:** Missing information, unclear methodology, outdated results, limited applicability, or other factors that may affect how the evidence can be interpreted.

* **Evidence Gaps:** Requirements for which available evidence is insufficient to support reliable evaluation and may require additional documentation, clarification, testing, or validation.

### **Step 5\. Evaluate and Interpret Evidence**

The main paper provides detailed guidance on Demonstrated Performance, Evidence Strength, and Evidence Gaps and explains how these concepts should be interpreted. This step applies that framework to the evidence collected for each Requirement. Focus on whether the evidence is relevant and applicable to the intended use and whether it supports a sufficiently reliable finding.

When evaluating the evidence, consider:

* **Performance:** What the evidence demonstrates relative to the established standard.

* **Applicability:** Whether the evidence reflects the intended use, users, populations, conditions, and system version.

* **Limitations:** Any factors that may affect the reliability or interpretation of the finding.

* **Remaining uncertainty:** Whether additional evidence or testing is needed before making a final determination.

If material uncertainty remains, return to Step 4 to gather additional evidence before proceeding to the final Requirement Determination.

### **Step 6\. Determine Requirement Status**

The main paper explains the distinction between an Evidence Gap and a final Requirement Determination, including the three possible determinations: Yes, No, and Insufficient Evidence. This step applies that framework after the district has completed its reasonable efforts to evaluate and resolve material uncertainty.

Before recording the final determination, confirm:

* **Evidence reviewed:** The available evidence has been evaluated against the established Performance Standard or Benchmark.

* **Evidence gaps addressed:** Reasonable efforts have been made to obtain additional information, testing, validation, or clarification where material uncertainty remained.

* **Requirement status:** The evidence supports a final Yes, No, or Insufficient Evidence determination.

* **Rationale documented:** The determination and the evidence supporting it are recorded in the Trustworthiness Checklist.

* **Remaining uncertainty:** Any material limitations or unresolved concerns are documented for consideration in the broader adoption decision
